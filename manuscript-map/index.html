Formats & ISBNs

One ISBN per format, never reuse one across paperback, hardcover, ebook, and audio. Buy ISBNs from Bowker at myidentifiers.com if you're in the US (a 10-pack is far cheaper per-ISBN than buying one at a time), or search "[your country] ISBN agency" elsewhere. KDP will also assign a free ISBN automatically, but that locks the edition's publisher-of-record to Amazon, skip it if you plan to sell wide (Apple Books, Kobo, etc).

Price is the retail amount readers pay. You can change it anytime, including for promotions.

Categorization

A standardized code that tells retailers and libraries which shelf your book belongs on. Look up the full list at bisg.org ("BISAC Subject Headings List"), or check a similar, already-published book's Amazon listing under "Product details."
Optional. Same lookup as Primary BISAC, pick the most specific code that still fits, a code too broad buries you in a crowded category.
Search terms a reader might actually type in, not marketing language. Type the start of a phrase into Amazon's search bar and see what autocomplete suggests, those are real searches people are making.
Be specific — "Adult readers who enjoy slow-burn romantic suspense" is more useful than just "Adult."

Descriptions

The version someone reads in half a second while scrolling. It needs to hook, not summarize.
Sells the book, it doesn't summarize the plot start to finish, and it should never reveal the ending. Look at bestsellers in your genre for tone and structure before writing your own.

Project Questionnaire

Answer once — clarity for positioning, metadata & marketing

Prefer to go one step at a time?

1. The Book

The version you'd say out loud if someone asked "what's your book about?" at a party. Not your final blurb, just a working answer to clarify your own thinking.
Not for readers, this is for you. It helps you stay motivated and surfaces the emotional core you'll want to lean into in marketing later.
2–3 already-published books close enough in tone or audience that "if you liked X, you'll like this" makes sense — one per line, with a short note on why each one fits. Browse a similar book's Amazon page under "Customers also bought" for real, reader-tested comps.
This becomes the core of your pitch. Without a clear answer here, your marketing tends to default to sounding like everyone else's.

2. The Reader

A specific person, not a demographic category. What they already read and where they spend time online matters more than age, and makes them far easier to actually reach with ads or content.
As specific as you can get — "psychological thriller" over just "thriller." This should generally match the BISAC categories in the Metadata Vault.
Readers rely on this to know what they're getting. Mismatched expectations are one of the most common sources of negative reviews.
The feeling a reader trusts you to deliver by the end. This should match what your comps above also promise.

3. Goals & Success

There's no wrong answer here — naming your real priority honestly shapes which of the other questions matter most.
Keep this realistic, not aspirational. Most first books sell modestly — an honest target here helps set an honest marketing budget below.
Should match the Publication Date in the Metadata Vault once that's set.
Something you could actually check against in a year — "500 copies sold and an email list of 300 readers," not just "sell lots of books."

4. Marketing & Reach

Be honest about size. This directly affects how much of your launch depends on paid ads versus people who already know you.
Check only what you'll actually do consistently. An ARC team you abandon after week one is worse than committing to fewer channels you'll actually maintain.
Covers things like ads, promo site submissions (BookBub, Written Word Media), or ARC reader incentives. "$0, organic only" is a completely valid answer.

5. Practical Reality

A realistic number, not an aspirational one. This affects which later stages (marketing, distribution, post-launch) are realistic to do yourself versus needing help with.
Where you're least confident or most likely to get stuck. Useful as a signal for where to budget for professional help first.
Not shown anywhere else. Exists to help surface what's actually slowing you down.
