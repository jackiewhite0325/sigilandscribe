When the Dice Roll · Book 1 · S.J. Helix

Chapter 3: Invoice Day

Read at your own pace. Come back when you want, leave when you need to.

The dice roll at 6:40 every morning.

Today they land on the first bounce, all of them, like they are not even sorry.

I sit up slow, because sitting up fast is how you find out what hurts, and I run the check. Ankles: left puffy, still, which makes three days running, a streak. Right: fine, and smug about it. Knees: click, click, no formal complaints. Hips: the left one is out.

Not slippy. Not threatening to go. Out, in earnest, sitting wrong in its socket like a guest who has looked at the room and decided to stay.

I push it back with the heel of my hand. It settles with the soft thunk, the one I feel more than hear, and then, because today is apparently a day for honesty, it starts aching immediately, deep and warm, like it wants credit for going back in.

"Rude," I tell it.

I drink the salt water before I stand, the whole jam jar, sitting down, because standing up first to see how the head feels is a rookie mistake and I am not a rookie. The head stays mostly where I left it. One column of the check comes back kind. The rest of the page is what it is.

I spent Monday and Tuesday like they were free. Wednesday has arrived with the bill.

I get dressed sitting on the bed, which takes longer and annoys me more, and I put the wrap on the ankle, and I go down the stairs one at a time, left side to the wall, hand on the rail, like a person in a safety pamphlet.

Minnie takes one look at me crossing the kitchen and says, "No."

"No what? I have not even asked for anything yet."

"School is not happening today." She is already reaching for the phone. "Your hip is doing the thing where you walk like the floor owes you money."

"It went back in."

"And it is telling on itself anyway. I can hear it from here." She calls the school and says "a flare" in the calm voice adults use on other adults, like my body is a weather event the office should plan around. Then she slides a plate at me. Eggs. Toast. The good butter. "Home day. Ice, exercises, couch. That is the whole schedule. I will write it on the wall if it makes you feel better."

"It would need underlining."

"I will underline it twice."

There is chalk on her cuff. White dust ground into the sleeve of her sweater, fine and bright, like she has been up at the schedule wall already this morning, except the wall is right there and I can see it from the table and nothing on it is new. Same seven columns. Same big Friday letters.

I file the chalk under Probably Nothing.

Muffin is asleep across the back doorway with her chin on the threshold, guarding the garden from a position of total unconsciousness. Every few breaths her ear twitches. Whatever she is dreaming about, she is winning.

"Your dog is blocking the door," I say.

"She is holding the perimeter," Minnie says, stepping over her with a laundry basket like this is a thing that happens every day, which, to be fair, it is.

Minnie eats standing up, the way she does when her mind is somewhere else, and her hand shakes a little around her mug, the tired kind of shake. She did not sleep last night either, I think. She looks dimmed down, like a lamp turned low, and today she is one notch lower.

I do not ask about the chalk. I do not ask about the mug. A home day gives you too much time to notice things, and I have been noticing things since 6:40, and the day is young.

The exercises happen on the living room rug, because the couch is for after, and rewards in this house are sequenced.

Minnie sets the ice pack rotation like a general. Ice on the hip for twenty minutes. Exercises. Ice on the ankle while I elevate it on the arm of the couch and she pretends not to watch me do the exercises, which she is absolutely watching.

"All of them," she says.

"Sixty percent."

"S.J."

"My hip is out, my ankle is a grapefruit, and I am being so brave about it. Sixty percent is generous. Sixty percent is a gift."

"Seventy-five."

"Sixty, and I will not complain during the ice, which is a lie, but it is a lie we can both enjoy."

She sighs the sigh of a woman who has been negotiating with me since I was a screaming baby in the woods and knows exactly how this ends. "Sixty. And the ice stays the full twenty."

Rep one is fine. Rep two is fine. Rep three is where Muffin arrives, having abandoned the perimeter, and lies down across my shins with the full sixty pounds and a sigh like she has worked a long shift.

"Your dog is on my legs."

"She is providing resistance training."

"She is providing dog."

"Resistance," Minnie says, "is where you find it."

I do the leg lifts around her. It is possible. It is not dignified. Muffin's tail thumps the rug every time my foot comes down near her, approving, and by rep eight I am laughing, which costs me the hip a little, and Minnie pretends to read her book with her eyes moving in a way that suggests the book is not the thing being read.

Sixty percent. She counts. I count. We arrive at the same number, which in this house counts as a treaty.

During the rest between sets, with the ice going numb and lovely on my hip, I look at the back door.

Above the door, on the frame, there are marks in chalk.

I have lived in this house my whole life. I have come through that door ten thousand times, with groceries, with school bags, with Muffin on a leash she considers a group project. I have never once looked at the top of the frame.

The marks are small. A knot of lines, chalked tight and careful, like a signature in a language I almost know. And they are fresh. That is the thing I notice from all the way across the room, through the kitchen doorway: the white is bright. Unsmudged. The edges are sharp. Chalk fades. Chalk gets dusted and bumped and rained on through the screen. This chalk went up recently, the way paint looks when it has just dried.

Minnie turns a page she is not reading.

I look back at my ankle like my ankle is fascinating.

I let Muffin out before lunch, because the perimeter is not going to hold itself and also because I want to look at the front door, and I am not proud of the order of those reasons.

The front door has one too.

Same knot of lines, chalked above the lintel, out of the weather under the porch roof. Fresh. Bright as a new notebook.

I stand on the porch in my wrap and my socks and look at it for a while. Fifteen years I have lived behind these doors. Level 1 wizards do not learn wards. Wards are third-year. The physio told me that once, making conversation: wards are for people whose magic holds still long enough to tie a knot in it. Mine does not hold still. Mine fights itself. Nobody has ever suggested teaching me to ward anything.

And yet the house is warded. Front and back. By someone whose magic holds still, who never mentioned it, who refreshed the chalk recently enough that the white is still bright, and who has chalk dust ground into her cuff at breakfast.

Out back, taking the long way around because now I am a person who walks around her own house checking it, I look at the garden gate.

There is a line of salt along the ground under the gate. Poured careful as icing, white and unbroken from post to post.

Muffin sniffs it once, with professional interest, and then goes the long way through the gap by the hedge, like the line is a door she has decided to respect. Dogs are not supposed to know about salt lines. I stand there in the garden with my hip aching in the cold and I do not step over it either.

At lunch I ask about exactly one thing, because I am pacing myself.

"What is the salt at the gate for?"

"Slugs," Minnie says. Promptly. Too promptly, the way the latch answer came yesterday. "They eat the garden down to stems if you let them."

"Slugs."

"It is a bad year for them."

It is not a bad year for anything, and it is barely slug season at all; the garden is mostly done and brown. Minnie looks at her plate. Minnie, who cannot lie to a telemarketer without apologizing to the phone afterward, sets her jaw and holds eye contact and serves me the slug story like it is carved on a tablet.

I eat my soup. I file it away: two doors, one gate, one ready-made lie.

The chalk, I do not ask about at all. Some questions you can feel the size of before you ask them, and that one has a size, and I am on a home day, and home days have a schedule: ice, exercises, couch.

Acupuncture is at two. It is the only item on today's schedule that involves leaving the house, and I hold onto it like a field trip.

The acupuncturist's room is quiet in the expensive way. A padded table. A clock that ticks like it is counting something it intends to collect. Air that smells faintly of herbs and clean cotton.

She has been sticking needles in me since I was eleven. I pretend not to like it. This is a tradition we maintain together, like the physio's cold hands: she asks how the hip is, I say "rude," she writes something down that I choose to believe is professional.

The needles go in along the hip and two in the ankle, thin as whispers. The trick of acupuncture, the part I pretend is not the trick, is that after about five minutes the ache stops being the loudest thing in the room. My body runs the check on its own, quieter. Hip: warm. Ankle: warm. Head: clear.

"You are holding yourself like a clenched fist today," she says.

"I am a delight."

"Mm." She puts a needle in near my wrist, for the general clenchedness, and then she picks up my hand, casual as anything, and turns it over. Palm up. She looks at my fingertips.

There is nothing to see. I checked in the car. The blue from last night is gone; I pressed it out of existence against the stair tread before bed and it stayed gone through the salt water and the ice. My hands look like hands.

"Anything new happening with your hands lately?" she asks.

Too casually. That is the only way to describe it. It is the voice of a person laying a card down face-up and watching your face instead of the card. Her eyes are on my hand and not on my face, which is worse, somehow. It means she has practiced where to look.

"They tingle," I say. "When I do magic. It has always done that."

"More than it used to?"

"Maybe," I say, because the spark jumped to the jam jar yesterday morning and the water rippled without a touch last night and my palms went hot on the stairs like I was holding something I had not picked up. "A little."

"Mm," she says, and nods, like that is an ordinary answer, like girls say that in this room every day of the week, and she sets my hand down gently and goes back to the hip, and asks me about school, and the moment closes like a door pulled to.

And that is the thing I cannot stop looking at, all the way home in the car, with Minnie humming and the bakery bag on the seat between us. Not the question. The nod. She asked about my hands the way you ask about rain when you have already seen the clouds. Like she already had a guess. Like she was checking her guess against my face.

The physio knows my hip better than I do. The acupuncturist asks about my hands like she is confirming something. The herbalist tomorrow will probably ask about my dreams.

I have spent fifteen years being the only person in this town who does not know what is happening to me, or so I thought. It is starting to look like I might just be the last one to be told.

At dinner Minnie pours the tea and her hand shakes, just the tired little shake, and she catches me watching and I look at my plate and she looks at her plate and we eat like that, two people being careful in a kitchen, and I decide that the questions can keep one more night. Whatever she is holding, she has been holding it a long time, and her hands are how it shows, and I am not going to be the thing that makes them shake worse tonight.

That is what I tell myself. It feels like kindness. It also feels like the thing I do instead of asking, which, now that I look at it, is something I do a lot.

Later, in bed, I run the inventory.

Hip: in. Holding a grudge, but in. Ankle: iced, wrapped for the night, elevated on a pillow like royalty. Head: clear. Magic: quiet all day, because I did not ask it for anything, and it did not ask me for anything, and we are both pretending that is an arrangement.

Chalk marks above the doors: two. Freshened.
Salt line at the gate: one. Unbroken.
Questions about my hands from people who were not supposed to have any: one. Too casual.
Things I said about any of it out loud: zero.

Muffin arranges herself across my legs with the full ceremony, three turns and a collapse, and sighs, and is asleep inside a minute, twitching in some dream where she is winning.

Minnie has been keeping this house like a held breath since before I can remember. Doors checked. Lines poured. Marks freshened in chalk above the frames when nobody is looking, the way she must have done it a thousand times before I ever thought to look up. I have lived inside it my whole life and never once saw the holding.

The house is quiet. The doors are marked. The gate is salted. Tomorrow the dice will roll again at 6:40, and the house will be defended, and I will be inside it, noticing.

And that has to count for something, even if nobody will tell me what it is counting against.
