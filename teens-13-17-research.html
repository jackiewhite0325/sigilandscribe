Research brief · ages 13 to 17

What teens need from a digital space

A practical foundation for a warm teen section: development, digital habits, disability inclusion, voice, and the role Muffin can play as a steady friend.

Respect before engagement

Trust their intelligence. Give them choices. Let them participate quietly or contribute meaningfully. Never confuse attention with care.

01

The developmental brief

Adolescence is not a waiting room for adulthood. It is a period of rapid learning, identity formation, heightened social awareness, and growing agency. A teen space should give young people room to try, choose, belong, and matter.[1]

  1. 1

    Identity exploration

    Offer low-stakes ways to test ideas, interests, roles, and values without locking anyone into a label.

  2. 2

    Real autonomy

    Give options, explain consequences plainly, and let teens decide how deeply they want to participate.

  3. 3

    Regulation through practice

    Emotion and reward systems can run ahead of planning skills. Interactive practice works better than lectures.

  4. 4

    Belonging and respect

    A respected role among peers and adults is a developmental need, especially when discrimination is already making the hill steeper.

  5. 5

    Meaning through contribution

    Teens benefit from doing something useful for others. Invite them to shape the space, not merely consume it.

  6. 6

    Action-based learning

    Trying, getting something wrong, and reflecting with support strengthens decision-making and self-trust.

  7. 7

    Rest without pressure

    Sleep supports development, learning, and mental health. Avoid engagement loops while keeping help easy to reach at night.

Design for a capable person who is still practicing, not a problem to be corrected.

02

What earns attention

The pattern is consistent: visual storytelling, relatable situations, friendship, hopeful framing, and participation that feels optional rather than demanding.

48.5%preferred animation over live action in a 2025 survey of 1,500 people ages 10 to 24.[2]
~60%of ages 14 to 24 wanted more stories with friendship at the center rather than romance.[2]
32.7%wanted stories that felt like their own lives, above fantasy, real-world issues, or aspiration in that same survey.[2]
46%of teens reported being online almost constantly in Pew’s December 2025 findings.[14]

Important scope note: several preference studies group teens with young adults. The direction is useful, but it is not a precise age-13-to-17 split.

Translate the evidence into experience

  • Story formatShort illustrated or animated moments, with text and captions always available.
  • Relationship centerPlatonic friendship, including cross-gender friendships without an automatic romance track.
  • ParticipationMood check-ins, quizzes, polls, and choices that return something useful, never a diagnosis.
  • Reading rhythmBite-sized sections, vertical scrolling, clear language, and permission to lurk without posting.
  • Emotional registerHonest about hard things, but never hopeless. Validate first, then offer options.
  • ControlNo pressure loops. Teens asked for more privacy, less pressure, and more control over their online experience.[17]
03

The missing pieces

Disabled, chronically ill, and neurodivergent teens are not a side audience. The strongest opportunity is to build the whole space around access, authentic representation, peer connection, and voice from the beginning.

2.4%

Representation

Only 2.4% of speaking characters in the top 100 films were depicted with any disability in cited 2024 data. Visible disability occupied under 1% of TV and streaming screen time.[4] Neurodivergent characters are often flattened into familiar stereotypes.

Build complex people with strengths, friction, humor, wants, and ordinary days.
Access

Accessibility

Guidance exists, but youth sites often lag: captions, meaningful alt text, short paragraphs, subdued color, minimal motion, keyboard access, and playback control.[5]

Treat accessibility as the starting structure, not a later patch.
1%

Voice

A 2025 review reported that only 1% of adolescent health research included adolescent voice.[18] The people with the most at stake are too often left out of design.

Create a continuing teen input loop with real influence and compensation.

Peer connection is not an extra

Disabled students use digital spaces to build relationships, explore disability identity, learn coping strategies, and control when and how they engage.[6] Chronically ill teens also need peers who understand absence, appointments, fatigue, and taking a growing role in their own care.[9] Racial and ethnic minority teens face additional gaps in access to therapy, medication, virtual care, and community support.[7]

04

Why Muffin belongs here

Muffin can bridge warmth and credibility if she is written as a companion, not a mascot who arrives to deliver a lesson.

A gentle friend who knows what it feels like to be judged by a label.

Research on teens and dogs links stronger companionship with more problem-solving, positive thinking, and emotional regulation, plus less rumination.[8]

Pitbull-type dogs also carry a powerful judged-by-appearance stigma. Shelter-label studies found that the label itself can change adoption outcomes even when appearance and observed behavior are comparable.[21]

  1. Let Muffin model, not preach.

    She can take a sensory break, rest without apologizing, breathe through a hard moment, or ask a friend to stay.

  2. Keep friendship at the center.

    Use loyal, varied, platonic bonds. A friend does not need to fix the problem to help.

  3. Use animation with purpose.

    Short reactions can make emotion visible: ears lower, breathing settles, posture softens. Keep motion optional and captioned.

  4. Make the anti-label theme light-touch.

    Show it through situations and choices. Let teens make the connection instead of turning Muffin into a moral.

  5. Protect the truth boundary.

    Dog companionship research is about real dogs generally. The special resonance of a pitbull character is a thoughtful inference, not a tested engagement result.

05

Six content pillars

These are strong starting structures for the teen section. Each one ties an observed need to a first buildable version.

  1. 01

    Peer-story library

    Short, first-person stories about school, friendship, disability, illness, identity, rest, and ordinary life. Reading peers’ stories can normalize and validate experience even when a teen never posts.[3]

    First version: three paid, consented stories, clearly labeled by topic and reading time.

  2. 02

    Mood check-ins

    Ask “What kind of day is it?” with an easy way to change an answer. Return a small set of options, not a score, label, or diagnosis.

    First version: a calm check-in with a confirmation step and three matched choices: stay, shift, or reach out.

  3. 03

    Coping micro-library

    Grounding, breathing, reframing, movement, sensory breaks, sleep support, and asking for help, each in a short, practical format.

    First version: six tools, each readable in under two minutes, with text, audio, and no autoplay.

  4. 04

    Teen contribution roles

    Invite art, writing, advice, accessibility review, and ongoing advisory input. Contribution supports purpose and a respected role.

    First version: a paid advisory review with a clear brief and a visible “changed because teens told us” log.

  5. 05

    Late-night friendly support

    Keep navigation calm and predictable at 2 a.m. Put crisis support within easy reach, but do not make every hard feeling look like an emergency.

    First version: a persistent help route, low-light palette, quiet audio defaults, and no streaks or endless feed.

  6. 06

    Accessibility from day one

    Captions, alt text, keyboard navigation, short paragraphs, scalable type, reduced motion, descriptive links, and no meaning carried by color alone.

    First version: make these launch requirements and test them with disabled and neurodivergent teens.

06

The five-point tone check

Use this before any teen-facing page, story, quiz, prompt, or piece of advice ships.

  1. Would a 15-year-old roll their eyes at this?
    If yes, rewrite it without trying to sound younger.
  2. Is the lesson embedded in a story, or lectured?
    Choose the story and trust the reader to understand.
  3. Am I curious or corrective?
    Ask and listen before assuming what needs fixing.
  4. Did a teen read this before it shipped?
    Make youth review part of the process, not a ceremonial last look.
  5. Does Muffin sound like a warm friend?
    She is a companion, not a mascot delivering morals.

Voice in practice

Lead with
“That makes sense.”
Then offer
“Want a few options?”
Use
Plain, specific, honest language.
Avoid
Faux-cool slang, pity, forced positivity, and “you should.”
Hold together
Strengths and struggles, without turning either into the whole person.
07

What still needs teen voices

The research gives a strong direction, not permission to skip co-design. These questions should stay open until teens help answer them.

Open questions

  • Which stories and tools feel most useful to 13 to 17 year olds specifically, rather than the broader 10-to-24 samples used in several studies?
  • Does Muffin’s pitbull identity resonate the way the stigma research suggests, or does another framing feel more natural to teens?
  • What balance of reading, animation, audio, and interaction works for teens with different energy, attention, sensory, and access needs?
  • How should private contribution, public contribution, moderation, consent, and compensation work in practice?
  • Which crisis resources are appropriate for the intended audience and geography at launch?

Recommended next move: sketch one peer story, one mood check-in, and one coping tool. Put those in front of a small, paid group of teens, including disabled, chronically ill, and neurodivergent teens, before naming the full teen section.
