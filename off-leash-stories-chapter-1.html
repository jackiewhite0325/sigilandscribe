When the Dice Roll · Book 1 · S.J. Helix

Chapter 1: The Morning Roll

Read at your own pace. Come back when you want, leave when you need to.

The dice roll at 6:40 every morning.

I do not mean real dice. I mean the ones inside my body, the ones nobody else can see, that decide what kind of day I get before my feet even touch the floor. Minnie says everybody wakes up and takes stock. She says it like it is the same thing. It is not the same thing.

This morning I sit up slow, because fast is how you find out, and I run the check. Ankles first. Left: tender, puffy around the bone, but it holds my weight when I press it into the mattress. It gets a wrap today, not the brace. Right: fine. Knees: both click, neither complains. Hips: the left one slides when I shift, a small wrong movement like a drawer that will not stay shut. I push it back with the heel of my hand and it settles with a soft thunk I feel more than hear.

Then I stand up.

The room tilts.

Okay. So today is a dizzy day. The blood does its thing where it forgets how to stay in my head when I am vertical, and the floor does a slow lean to the left like it is trying to tell me a secret. I stand still and count to ten and drink the salt water Minnie leaves on my nightstand every night in a jam jar, because she is the kind of person who believes in jam jars for everything. It tastes like the ocean decided to be a beverage. It helps. A little. Enough.

"Left hip's out again," I say to the ceiling. "And the tilt-a-whirl is open for business."

From downstairs, Minnie calls back: "Wrap or brace?"

"Wrap."

"Compression socks?"

I look at my feet. They look very far away. "Yes," I say, to the ceiling, because the ceiling is not judging me.

This is the norm. This is every morning. Some girls wake up and check their phones. I wake up and check my joints.

Minnie has the schedule wall in the kitchen. It is a chalkboard the size of a door, divided into seven columns, and every column is full. Monday: Dr. Ansel, joints, 9 AM. Tuesday: salt loading and the physio with the cold hands. Wednesday: acupuncture, which I pretend not to like. Thursday: the herbalist, who smells like a forest floor and charges like a surgeon. Friday: rest, which Minnie writes in big letters and underlines twice, like the chalk can make it true.

"Eat," Minnie says, sliding a plate across the table. Eggs, toast, the good butter. She is already dressed, hair pinned up, and she looks tired in the way she always looks tired, like someone turned her brightness down one notch and left it there. Minnie is small and sharp-featured, with gray in her dark hair that she earned, and hands that shake just a little when she is tired. She never talks about being tired.

"You have the wrap on?" she asks.

"Yes."

"Socks?"

"Yes."

"Did you actually drink the salt water or did you negotiate with it?"

"I drank it," I say. "Mostly. Seventy percent. That is a passing grade."

She gives me the look. The look says she found me in the woods when I was a baby, screaming loud enough to scare the crows, and she has been negotiating with me ever since, and she is better at it than I am.

Muffin chooses this exact moment to steal my toast.

She does it with the confidence of a criminal mastermind. One second the toast is on my plate, the next second there is a black-and-white blur, and then Muffin is sitting under the table with the toast in her mouth, her white face tilted up, the big black patch over her left eye making her look like a tiny bandit, her amber eyes doing the thing where they say I have no idea how this happened and I am the victim here.

"Muffin," I say.

She thumps her tail against the table leg. Thump. Thump. Thump. The toast does not move from her mouth.

"She is helping," Minnie says solemnly. "She can tell you need the protein more than the carbs."

"She is a toast thief."

"She is a toast thief who loves you."

I reach under the table. Muffin, reading the situation with the tactical genius of her breed, immediately rolls onto her back, toast still in her mouth, all four paws in the air, belly exposed. It is impossible to be angry at a dog who is weaponizing her own belly. I scratch it. She groans like an old man. The toast is gone. I accept this.

"School," Minnie says. "Bag packed?"

"Yes."

"Elevator, not stairs."

I do not answer fast enough.

"S.J."

"The stairs are faster," I say.

"The stairs are how you spend Thursday on the couch with an ice pack," she says. "Elevator."

"Fine."

She knows. She always knows. This is the other part of the norm: the negotiation. I push, she catches it, I pretend I was not pushing. I am fifteen and I have the self-preservation instincts of a moth near a porch light.

Mossgrove is a border town, which means nobody is quite sure where the human side ends and the fae side begins, and everybody pretends this is fine. The market street has a bakery next to a stall that sells charms against the evil eye, and nobody thinks this is strange. The school is a long low building with a courtyard in the middle, and the courtyard has a tree that is definitely, obviously fae, because it blooms in whatever color it feels like that week. This week it is gold. Last week it was a blue that made everyone a little seasick.

I take the elevator. I hate the elevator. It is slow and it smells like floor wax and everyone who sees me get out of it does the quick glance at my legs, checking for the thing that is wrong with me, and then the quicker glance away. There is nothing to see. That is the worst part. If I had a cast or a boot, people would understand. Instead I just look like a girl who takes the elevator for no reason.

My locker is on the second floor. By third period my left hip is sending up flares, little hot pulses with every step, and I am doing the thing where I stand very still between classes and pretend I am reading the announcements board. The announcements board says the autumn dance is in three weeks. The announcements board says tryouts for the debate team are Thursday. The announcements board does not say anything useful, like where a person is supposed to stand when her body is filing complaints.

Parker Wells goes past in his wheelchair.

He used to be the fastest thing on the football field. Everybody in Mossgrove knows the story: the break, the surgery, the months in the big hospital two towns over, the friends who visited for two weeks and then stopped. He is still good-looking in the way that makes it worse, jawline and all, and he still has the ego, tilted chin, eyes straight ahead like the hallway is his. He does not look at me. Why would he. I am the girl with the invisible wrongness who takes the elevator. He is Parker Wells.

My chest does the stupid thing anyway.

In fourth period, Ms. Abernathy puts us in groups for the history project. I get sorted with two girls who have known each other since they were seven and a boy who is asleep with his eyes open. The girls do the polite thing, the scootch-over, the we have room, and I sit at the edge of their circle and laugh at the right times and contribute exactly one useful fact about border treaties, and the whole time there is a glass wall between me and them, and I am on the wrong side of it. They are nice. That almost makes it worse. If they were mean I could be angry. Instead I just sit there, adjacent to belonging, close enough to see it.

At lunch I sit with my back against the gold-blooming tree and read, and Muffin is not here because dogs are not allowed at school, which is frankly a policy failure, and I eat my sandwich and watch the courtyard and think about how there is nowhere I quite fit. Not with the fae kids, who can smell the human on me, or whatever it is they smell. Not with the human kids, who can smell the weird on me. Minnie says I am being dramatic. Minnie found me in the woods and chose me every day since, and somehow I still spend lunch periods feeling like a library book somebody shelved in the wrong section.

The hip holds. The dizziness fades by afternoon, the way it does, like the tide going out. By the last bell I almost feel like a regular person. This is the trap. This is always the trap.

The physio has cold hands. I have decided this is a job requirement.

She glances at the chart on the counter first. S.J. Helix, printed neat as a stranger's name, like it belongs to somebody more official than me.

"How is the hip?" she asks, pressing her arctic fingers into the joint while I lie on the table and stare at the ceiling tiles.

"Slippy," I say.

"Did you brace it?"

"I wrapped it."

"Did you take the elevator?"

"...Yes."

She gives me a look that says she does not believe me. She is right not to believe me. I took the elevator this morning and the stairs after lunch because the elevator was full and I did not want to wait and be stared at. The hip is filing a formal protest about the stairs. I do not mention the stairs.

She works the joint back into place with a practiced push, and it goes with the soft thunk, and the relief is immediate and embarrassing, like I have been holding my breath for hours and did not know it. She gives me exercises. I will do half of them. We both know I will do half of them.

Minnie waits in the car, engine running, a paper bag from the bakery on the passenger seat. "How'd it go?"

"She fixed the drawer," I say, climbing in. "I am doing half the exercises."

"Do three-quarters."

"Sixty percent. Final offer."

Muffin is in the back seat. The moment I sit down, she launches herself over the seatback like a furry missile and lands across my lap, all sixty pounds of her, and then she sighs, a huge theatrical pitbull sigh, and goes boneless. Her head hangs off my knee. Her black ear flops inside out. She is, objectively, the least dignified creature in the borderlands.

"She missed you," Minnie says.

"She missed the toast," I say, but I am already scratching behind the inside-out ear, and Muffin's back leg starts thumping against the door, and Minnie is smiling at the road, and for a second the car smells like fresh bread and dog, and it is such a small good thing that I hold onto it too tight.

Wizardry lessons happen at dusk, in the back garden, because Minnie says magic likes the in-between hours. I am a Level 1 learned wizard, which is the official term for someone who knows three spells and can reliably do one of them. My reliable one is lighting a candle. This is less impressive than it sounds.

"Stillness first," Minnie says. She settles onto the garden bench with the careful slowness of someone whose body charges interest on every movement. "You cannot shape what you cannot hold."

I sit cross-legged on the grass. Muffin sits next to me, then reconsiders, then lies down with her chin on my wrapped ankle, which is either comfort or her claiming the ankle as territory. With Muffin it is always both.

The exercise is simple. Breathe. Be still. Feel the magic the way you feel a room when someone walks into it, there and then there. Then, when you are ready, reach for the candle and ask the flame to come.

I breathe. I am good at the breathing part. Four years of being told to breathe through things has made me excellent at breathing.

I reach.

The candle flares.

Not lights. Flares, a sudden whoosh of flame a foot high, bright and wrong, and I yank my hand back, and the flame gutters down to nothing, and the candle is out, and there is a scorch mark on the saucer, and my hands are tingling like I grabbed a nettle.

Minnie does not flinch. She never flinches. "Again," she says quietly. "Smaller this time. You reached with your whole arm. Reach with a finger."

This is the other thing nobody tells you about my magic. It does not come out right. It has never come out right. It surges and sputters, too much and then nothing, like a faucet somebody installed backwards. Minnie says every wizard's magic has its own shape and mine is just still finding it. She says it kindly. I hear the part she does not say: yours is the only one I have ever seen that fights itself.

I try again. Breathe. Reach with a finger.

The wick catches. A small, ordinary flame, trembling in the dusk air. It holds.

"Good," Minnie says, and there is real warmth in it. "Hold it. Just hold it."

I hold it. The flame steadies. The garden goes quiet around us, the good kind of quiet, and Muffin's breathing slows against my ankle, and Minnie starts humming under her breath, some old tune with no words, and for one breath, one whole breath, everything holds. The flame. The quiet. The dog. The woman who found me in the woods and never once acted like I was anything but hers.

Then Muffin farts.

It is long. It is resonant. It echoes faintly off the garden wall. Muffin lifts her head, looks directly at Minnie, and thumps her tail once, with unmistakable pride.

The flame goes out. Minnie makes a sound like a teakettle giving up. I am laughing so hard my hip protests, and Muffin, delighted by the audience, farts again, and Minnie says, "That dog is a menace," in the exact tone she uses for people she adores, and the lesson is over, and it does not matter, because for one breath everything held.

Later, in bed, I run the inventory. Hip: settled, aching dully. Ankle: puffy but fine. Head: clear. The candle thing: I lit it. Eventually. The toast: lost to a superior opponent. Parker Wells: still out of my league, still the thing my chest does the stupid thing about. The group project: I contributed one fact about border treaties. The tree: gold this week.

Minnie knocks on the doorframe. "Acupuncture tomorrow," she says. "I wrote it in big letters. You will pretend not to like it."

"I saw the underlining."

"Underlining means it is law."

"Noted."

She comes in and sits on the edge of the bed, and Muffin immediately relocates from the floor to the bed, stepping on every sensitive part of me on the way up, and then she circles three times and collapses across my legs like a sandbag, and Minnie and I both pretend this is normal.

"You did good today," Minnie says.

"I took the stairs after lunch."

"I know."

"The physio knew too."

"Everybody knew, sweetheart. You are about as sneaky as the dog."

Muffin, in her sleep, kicks me in the ribs.

Minnie brushes my hair back from my forehead, her hand cool and light. "Stillness first," she says softly. "You cannot shape what you cannot hold. But you are learning to hold, S.J. I see it."

She turns off the light. The door clicks. Muffin snores, a wet rhythmic buzzsaw, and the house settles around us, and tomorrow the dice will roll again at 6:40, and I have no idea what they will say.

But tonight, for one breath, everything held.

And that has to count for something.
